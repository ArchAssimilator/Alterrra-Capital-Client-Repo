# **Project Savannah: Famous Brands Limited (JSE: FBR) Take-Private LBO Model Assumptions and Market Intelligence**

## **Step 1 — Macro and sector context**

* **South African nominal GDP growth consensus (2025–2030):** The International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the South African National Treasury project real gross domestic product (GDP) growth to accelerate from 1.3% in 2025 to 1.8% by the end of the decade, underpinned by resilient consumption and the compounding benefits of structural infrastructure reforms.1 Concurrently, the South African Reserve Bank (SARB) forecasts headline inflation to converge firmly toward the midpoint of its revised 3.0% target band over the medium term.1 Synthesizing these dual macroeconomic pillars yields an aggregate sovereign nominal GDP growth consensus trajectory anchored between 4.3% and 4.8% per annum across the 2025–2030 forecast window.  
* **SA Consumer Price Index (food component) forecast for 2025–2027:** The domestic food inflation environment is currently undergoing robust stabilization, recording an annualized rate of 4.4% entering early 2026, significantly decelerating from previous cyclical peaks.5 Advanced econometric models project the South African food CPI to moderate further, trending downward to approximately 3.20% in 2027 and 3.00% in 2028\.6 This deflationary momentum is heavily supported by sharp price contractions in cereal products (with white rice demonstrating 11.0% deflation) and global grain stabilization, which collectively offset acute, localized inflationary spikes within the domestic red meat and beef complexes.5  
* **QSR and casual dining sector growth rate in SA and sub-Saharan Africa:** The South African foodservice ecosystem is experiencing aggressive, structural market expansion, projected by Mordor Intelligence to scale from a baseline valuation of USD 10.16 billion in 2025 to USD 20.11 billion by 2030, representing a remarkable compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 14.63%.8 Within this broader aggregate, the traditional Quick Service Restaurant (QSR) segment—which currently commands 48.16% of total market share—is forecast to achieve a baseline value CAGR of 7.61%, while highly digitized, capital-efficient segments such as cloud kitchens are projected to expand at an accelerated 17.41% CAGR.8  
* **Key risks to the consumer spending outlook:**  
  * *Load-shedding resolution:* While severe electrical grid constraints have largely dissipated by late 2025, structural fragility in energy transmission remains a latent, systemic risk to continuous cold-chain logistics, raw material manufacturing, and unit-level restaurant operating margins.10  
  * *Formal employment rate:* Structurally entrenched high unemployment, deteriorating physical infrastructure, and constrained household disposable income restrict discretionary casual dining frequency, forcing a persistent margin-eroding shift toward high-volume, value-oriented QSR offerings.12  
  * *Rand depreciation:* Heightened currency volatility directly inflates the cost of imported agricultural inputs—notably wheat, palm oil, and specialized mechanically deboned poultry cuts—threatening corporate gross margin stability if forward hedging strategies prove inadequate.14  
* **Key tailwinds:**  
  * *Infrastructure recovery:* The implementation of the Independent Transmission Project (aiming to expand transmission infrastructure by 14,000 kilometers) and a R200 billion capital injection into port and freight-rail networks are systematically reducing systemic supply chain friction and lowering baseline logistics costs.10  
  * *Digital ordering penetration:* E-commerce delivery channels are structurally rebasing unit economics, with delivery logistics growing at a 16.34% CAGR, supported by low-data messaging ordering systems and expanded third-party logistics networks penetrating high-density township nodes.8  
  * *Franchise model resilience:* The fundamentally asset-light nature of franchised ecosystems shields the central holding company from direct, localized wage inflation and unit-level capital expenditure requirements, ensuring robust, highly predictable free cash flow generation during periods of severe macroeconomic distress.17

## **Step 2 — Operating assumption benchmarks**

## **Q3: Revenue growth rate (YoY)**

* **Benchmark range:** 5.6% to 11.2% 19 (March 2026).  
* **Base case recommendation:** 6.5%  
* **Upside:** 8.5%  
* **Downside:** 4.2%  
* **One-line rationale:** The 6.5% base case aligns with Famous Brands' recent mid-single-digit turnover growth and the systemic shift toward value-oriented QSRs, heavily discounting the double-digit sector aggregate to reflect mature market penetration and cautious consumer discretionary spending.

The South African quick-service restaurant and broader casual dining sector presents a highly bifurcated revenue environment, necessitating a granular separation of organic unit expansion, menu price inflation, and inorganic acquisition activity. Historical financial disclosures from Famous Brands for the six months ending August 2025 indicate a localized, organic turnover growth of 5.6%, reaching ZAR 4.23 billion.21 This internal performance metric functions as the most immediate, unadulterated proxy for organic network expansion and pricing power within the domestic market. Conversely, direct competitor Spur Corporation (JSE: SUR) registered a significantly higher consolidated revenue expansion of 11.2% for the financial year ending June 2025, reaching ZAR 3.86 billion.19 However, parsing this growth reveals that it was aggressively buoyed by a 46.3% increase in retail company store sales following the strategic integration of the Doppio Collection, alongside a 10.1% increase in centralized manufacturing revenue.19 Spur's franchised restaurant turnover, which serves as a purer indicator of system-wide consumer demand and footprint growth, advanced by a more normalized 8.3% to ZAR 11.5 billion.22

Further contextualizing the domestic peer group, Grand Parade Investments (JSE: GPL) demonstrates extreme revenue volatility due to extensive corporate restructuring, asset disposals, and its transition toward an investment holding structure, rendering its historical revenue metrics anomalous for operational benchmarking.20 On a global scale, multinational QSR conglomerates such as Restaurant Brands International (RBI) and Yum\! Brands exhibit massive systemic scale, but typically report organic system sales growth in the mid-single digits, reflecting the inherent limitations of mature market penetration.24

The base case recommendation of 6.5% is meticulously constructed to strip out the aggressive, M\&A-driven inorganic growth observed in peer comparables like Spur Corporation. It relies on the synthesis of a 3.2% to 3.6% blended menu price inflation—mirroring the broader food CPI stabilization environment—paired with a conservative 2.9% to 3.3% organic volume and footprint expansion. This volume expansion is anticipated to be predominantly driven by Famous Brands' Signature Brands portfolio and its strategic geographical expansion into the Rest of Africa and Middle East (AME) segments, which operate from a lower penetration base.

The upside scenario of 8.5% captures the potential for accelerated market share capture if the newly implemented digital ordering applications, cloud kitchen formats, and optimized township delivery logistics execute flawlessly. This upside aligns closely with Spur's baseline franchise network growth of 8.3%, suggesting that operational parity in aggressive footprint expansion could yield equivalent top-line results. Conversely, the downside scenario of 4.2% models a severe stagnation in real volume growth. In this environment, revenue expansion relies entirely on inflationary price adjustments passed onto an increasingly distressed consumer base. If formal employment metrics deteriorate and disposable income is further squeezed by restrictive monetary policy, dining frequency will contract, limiting revenue growth to mere inflationary survival rather than real economic expansion.

## **Q4: Cost of sales (% of revenue)**

* **Benchmark range:** 32.5% to 66.2% 19 (March 2026).  
* **Base case recommendation:** 56.5%  
* **Upside:** 55.0%  
* **Downside:** 58.5%  
* **One-line rationale:** Famous Brands' vertically integrated manufacturing and logistics pillars structurally dictate a higher cost of sales relative to pure-play franchisors; the 56.5% base case integrates expected deflation in grain inputs against elevated domestic meat pricing.

Conducting cost of sales analysis in the franchised restaurant sector requires a strict, structural demarcation between pure intellectual property franchisors and vertically integrated operators. Global QSR benchmarks, such as Restaurant Brands International, report gross margins ranging from 33.8% to 41.4% (translating to a cost of sales between 58.6% and 66.2%), depending heavily on the consolidation ratio of corporate-owned units versus franchised locations.25 Domestically, Spur Corporation operates with a highly expansive gross margin of approximately 32.47% (indicating a cost of sales of 67.53%), a figure heavily influenced by its blended model of high-margin franchise fee extraction and wholesale distribution.19 Famous Brands operates a profoundly different, highly capital-intensive structural model. Its "Brands, Manufacturing, Logistics, and Retail" matrix means it manufactures and physically distributes massive volumes of perishable proteins, sauces, bakery products, and mineral water to its localized network.12 Historically, Famous Brands has yielded a consolidated gross margin of roughly 43.00%, placing its true operational cost of sales securely at 57.0%.26

The fundamental macroeconomic drivers of this input cost base are currently undergoing a severe structural transition across the South African agricultural value chain. On the deflationary side, global and local agricultural commodities are easing significantly. South Africa's grain harvest recovery has suppressed yellow maize and soybean prices, which constitute approximately 70% of the input costs for poultry feed.14 Consequently, the USDA forecasts a 2% expansion in South African chicken meat production in 2026, reaching 1.68 million tons, strictly driven by these lower feed costs and the industry's recovery from Highly Pathogenic Avian Influenza (HPAI).15 Furthermore, white rice prices remain in deep deflation at \-11.0% year-on-year, and aggregate cereal products inflation has plunged to a mere 0.6%.5

However, these systemic supply chain gains are violently offset by crises within the red meat complex. Beef prices in South Africa are acutely elevated due to the ongoing spread of foot-and-mouth disease (FMD), which continues to heavily constrain slaughter numbers and delay herd rebuilding efforts. Consequently, beef steak, stewing beef, and mince inflation reached 31.2%, 30.3%, and 28.0% respectively in January 2026\.5 Pork prices have similarly spiked to 19.5%.5

The base case recommendation of 56.5% assumes that Famous Brands' centralized procurement scale and deep vertical integration will allow it to successfully arbitrage the declining grain and poultry costs to absorb the shocks in the beef supply chain, resulting in a marginal 50-basis-point improvement over historical averages. The 55.0% upside scenario materializes if global grain prices collapse further, domestic meat supplies normalize faster than anticipated following accelerated FMD vaccination protocols, and the Rand strengthens to suppress the cost of imported specialized poultry cuts.14 The 58.5% downside reflects a severe failure in this corporate hedging strategy, particularly if a rapidly depreciating Rand exacerbates the cost of imported inputs or if global oil prices drive up agricultural production costs.

## **Q7: Administration expenses (% of revenue)**

* **Benchmark range:** 6.3% 19 (March 2026).  
* **Base case recommendation:** 2.8%  
* **Upside:** 2.4%  
* **Downside:** 3.5%  
* **One-line rationale:** Given the group's massive revenue denominator encompassing bulk logistics and manufacturing, administration expenses as a percentage of total turnover run exceptionally low; the base case absorbs anticipated 4.5% formal sector wage inflation.

Benchmarking General and Administrative (G\&A) expenses for Famous Brands against standard retail and restaurant metrics requires substantial adjustment due to the sheer scale of the company's supply chain and manufacturing revenue. Spur Corporation reported administration expenses of ZAR 246 million against a consolidated revenue of ZAR 3.86 billion, translating to an administrative burden of approximately 6.3%.19 However, Famous Brands generates more than double that total revenue (reporting approximately ZAR 8.28 billion annually) 29, operating vast, high-volume, low-margin logistics and manufacturing divisions. This structural reality highly inflates the revenue denominator without necessitating a proportional, linear increase in head-office executive compensation, legal staffing, and administrative overhead, structurally depressing the G\&A ratio as a percentage of total turnover.

The primary driver for the administrative cost base is head-office and centralized staff remuneration. The South African macroeconomic landscape dictates a sticky, upwardly mobile wage inflation environment. Retaining specialized human capital, particularly in corporate finance, franchise management, and food science, remains highly competitive amid localized skill shortages. Leading actuarial and compensation consultancies, such as Axiomatic, project a mean South African corporate salary increase of 4.50% for 2026\.4 Simultaneously, the statutory national minimum wage was elevated by 5% to ZAR 30.23 per hour effective March 2026\.32 While the minimum wage primarily impacts unit-level franchised operations rather than the corporate holding company, it establishes a baseline wage floor that cascades upward into corporate distribution center administrative staff and entry-level corporate functions.

Applying a 4.5% organic growth rate to the administrative cost pool against a 6.5% top-line revenue expansion suggests a natural margin accretion and a lowering of the percentage ratio. However, the imperative to aggressively digitize backend systems, optimize cloud-based enterprise resource planning (ERP) software, and invest in data-driven supply chain management tools will consume early efficiency gains. Therefore, the 2.8% base case allows for necessary strategic reinvestment in administrative technological infrastructure while absorbing the 4.5% wage inflation benchmark. The upside scenario of 2.4% models a flawless execution of corporate restructuring, where artificial intelligence and automation in administrative processing yield immediate, tangible reductions in headcount requirements without sacrificing operational oversight. The downside scenario of 3.5% accounts for potential operational friction, including costly severance packages during restructuring, aggressive unionized wage settlements that outpace the 4.5% inflation forecast, or increased legal and compliance costs associated with heightened regulatory scrutiny.

## **Q8: Marketing expenses (% of revenue)**

* **Benchmark range:** 4.0% to 9.0% 19 (March 2026).  
* **Base case recommendation:** 8.5%  
* **Upside:** 7.5%  
* **Downside:** 9.5%  
* **One-line rationale:** Marketing spend is strictly governed by ring-fenced franchise agreements; the 8.5% base case incorporates the necessary shift from broad-based television to higher-ROI, hyper-localized digital and performance media spend.

Marketing expenses within franchised operations possess a unique, highly specific accounting architecture that frequently distorts standard margin analysis. Funds are typically extracted via a contractually fixed percentage levy applied directly to gross franchisee turnover, pooled at the corporate level into restricted accounts, and subsequently deployed for brand building. Consequently, the marketing revenue and marketing expense lines frequently mirror each other closely, neutralizing direct net profit impact, provided the corporate fund does not operate in a deficit and require holding company subsidization.34 Spur Corporation, for example, reported marketing expenses of ZAR 347.78 million against a total revenue of ZAR 3.86 billion, equating to an effective rate of 9.0%.19 It must be noted that Spur’s revenue includes ZAR 385.1 million in specifically designated marketing fund revenue 19, validating the thesis that these expenses scale directly and proportionally to the franchised network turnover.

The strategic allocation of this massive capital pool is undergoing a profound paradigm shift across the African continent. Traditional, broad-reach channels—linear television, print media, and static out-of-home (OOH) billboards—are rapidly ceding ground to algorithmic, programmatic digital environments. Forecasts indicate that digital ad spend in Africa will expand by 12.5% annually, reaching USD 4.3 billion by 2026, with retail media and social platforms leading the reallocation.33 In South Africa, YouTube's ad reach alone penetrates over 41.4% of the total population.36 The proliferation of data-rich mobile connectivity enables hyper-segmented, performance-driven marketing that yields demonstrably higher returns on invested capital compared to legacy campaigns. Fast-food operators are increasingly reallocating budgets toward targeted social media, localized search engine marketing, and integrated promotions within dominant digital delivery platforms like Uber Eats and Mr D.8

The base case recommendation of 8.5% reflects the sustained competitive intensity of the South African QSR environment. Defending market share against aggressive international entrants requires vast capital deployment, and the transition to digital media, while offering higher ROI, requires substantial upfront investment in digital asset creation and data analytics capabilities. The upside scenario of 7.5% materializes if digital media buying efficiencies allow the group to voluntarily lower the required franchisee marketing levy, thereby indirectly boosting unit-level franchisee profitability and mitigating the risk of future corporate royalty reductions. The downside risk of 9.5% reflects a scenario where intense sector competition forces Famous Brands to subsidize the marketing fund directly from corporate reserves to maintain brand visibility, breaking the ring-fenced neutrality of the fund and directly eroding corporate operating margins.

## **Q9: Operations expenses (% of revenue)**

* **Benchmark range:** 4.1% to 11.2% 19 (March 2026).  
* **Base case recommendation:** 20.5%  
* **Upside:** 19.5%  
* **Downside:** 22.0%  
* **One-line rationale:** Serving as the central nervous system of the group, the immense logistics and transport apparatus dictates a high operations expense ratio, benefiting presently from consecutive fuel price deflation and stabilized grid energy.

The operations expense variable represents both the core vulnerability and the primary competitive moat of the Famous Brands vertically integrated model. Standard pure-play intellectual property franchisors exhibit negligible operations expenses; for instance, Spur Corporation’s operations expenses registered at just 4.1% of revenue (ZAR 162.2 million).19 Famous Brands, conversely, operates a vast physical supply chain moving thousands of tons of perishable goods across multiple geographic borders daily. Macroeconomic benchmarks derived from the State of Logistics survey suggest that total logistics costs in South Africa equate to approximately 11.2% of GDP.38 For a vertically integrated food producer, where continuous temperature-controlled warehousing and highly localized, high-frequency delivery routes are non-negotiable operational requirements, logistics and operational transport costs represent a heavily disproportionate share of the overall cost structure.

The forward-looking operational environment for 2026 displays highly favorable, deflationary characteristics that should support margin expansion. The domestic fuel price index contracted by a significant 3.7% over the trailing 12 months ending January 2026\.5 Furthermore, the near-total elimination of systemic load-shedding has drastically curtailed the exigent operational costs previously incurred through the continuous operation of industrial diesel generators across the manufacturing and cold-storage network.11 The macroeconomic stabilization of the Rand, trading closer to ZAR 16.00 to the US Dollar, further suppresses the imported cost of commercial vehicle fleet maintenance, refrigeration components, and replacement parts.4

The base case recommendation of 20.5% assumes that these external tailwinds will partially offset the aggressive structural inflation inherent in commercial real estate leasing, third-party warehousing escalation, and massive municipal utility tariff hikes. The upside scenario of 19.5% models the successful, systemic integration of AI-driven route optimization software and automated non-temperature-controlled warehousing efficiencies, which currently constitute 91.25% of warehousing revenue share.39 This scenario also assumes sustained rand strength and continued fuel price suppression. The downside risk of 22.0% accounts for a potential, severe resurgence in global crude oil prices—driven by escalating geopolitical instability in the Middle East and Iranian conflicts 40—flowing directly into the group's massive commercial diesel consumption. Furthermore, any deterioration in the performance of the national port and rail infrastructure could force an over-reliance on expensive, road-based freight forwarding, shattering operational efficiency margins.

## **Q15: Effective tax rate**

* **Benchmark range:** 27.0% statutory 42 (March 2026).  
* **Base case recommendation:** 27.5%  
* **Upside:** 27.0%  
* **Downside:** 28.5%  
* **One-line rationale:** The South African statutory corporate tax rate remains frozen at 27.0%; the structural 50-basis-point premium in the base case accounts for non-deductible expenditures and cross-border operational friction.

The South African fiscal architecture has demonstrated remarkable stability regarding corporate taxation, prioritizing structural certainty over aggressive rate manipulation. The National Treasury, under the purview of Finance Minister Enoch Godongwana, systematically reduced the statutory corporate income tax rate from 28% to 27% effective for years of assessment commencing on or after 1 April 2022\. The 2026 National Budget unequivocally confirmed that the headline rate remains firmly anchored at 27.0% for the 2026/2027 assessment period.42 Forward-looking analysis from leading accounting advisory firms indicates zero political or fiscal appetite for further headline tax cuts; the government's strategic revenue focus remains strictly confined to administrative efficiency, bracket creep, technological enforcement through the South African Revenue Service (SARS), and combating illicit financial flows.44

Consequently, modeling the effective tax rate for a complex entity like Famous Brands requires moving beyond the statutory headline and meticulously examining the structural frictions of a multinational franchise operation. Famous Brands realizes diverse income streams from associate companies, master-license fees across the SADC region, and the repatriation of profits from the United Kingdom operations. Minor transfer pricing adjustments, non-deductible legal and corporate advisory fees associated with potential M\&A activity, and the ongoing implementation of Phase 2 carbon tax parameters naturally create an asymmetric upward pressure on the final effective tax burden.44

The base case of 27.5% represents a normalized, steady-state tax environment acknowledging these minor cross-border leakages and non-deductible corporate overheads. The 27.0% upside assumes perfect structural tax efficiency, aggressive optimization of available investment allowances, and the absolute absence of any withholding tax friction or transfer pricing penalties from international operations. The downside scenario of 28.5% anticipates aggressive, data-driven revenue collection enforcement by SARS. As the revenue service expands its technological capabilities and intensifies its focus on cross-border activity and transfer pricing justifiability, multinational franchise groups face the risk of a higher proportion of disallowed operational deductions and increased compliance costs, structurally dragging the effective tax rate upward.44

## **Q21: Terminal growth rate**

The terminal growth rate must accurately reflect the mature, perpetual cash flow expansion capability of Famous Brands following the expiration of the discrete five-year projection period (post-FY 2030). Standard valuation doctrine mandates that perpetual growth cannot indefinitely exceed the nominal GDP growth of the sovereign economy in which the asset operates, as doing so implies the entity will eventually eclipse the sovereign economy itself.

The International Monetary Fund's Article IV consultation and the South African National Treasury's Medium Term Budget Policy Statement definitively anchor long-run real GDP expectations. South African real GDP is forecast to reach 1.8% by the end of the decade, supported by structural reforms and resilient consumption.1 Concurrently, the SARB is actively enforcing monetary discipline to converge core inflation to the targeted 3.0% midline.1 Combining these parameters establishes a foundational, sovereign nominal GDP growth baseline of 4.8% for the terminal period.

However, the consumer foodservice sector fundamentally operates with a pricing power premium over the blended macroeconomic basket. Given the 14.63% CAGR projected for the South African foodservice sector leading into 2030, the continuous formalization of the informal economy, and sustained urbanization trends, the QSR sector demonstrates the capacity to expand at a structural premium to baseline nominal GDP.8 Furthermore, Famous Brands' deep brand equity and vertical integration allow for superior price elasticity relative to generic consumer goods. Consequently, an operational premium of approximately 70 basis points over the sovereign nominal baseline is analytically justified.

* **Base Case:** 5.5% (Representing structural alignment with nominal GDP plus a conservative pricing power premium derived from brand loyalty and supply chain dominance).  
* **Upside:** 6.5% (Assuming exceptional execution of demographic capture, aggressive regional African expansion shielding against low SA domestic growth, and superior digital logistics scaling that drastically outpaces standard macroeconomic indicators).  
* **Downside:** 4.5% (Assuming total macroeconomic stagnation where the group fails to outpace base inflation and suffers severe volume erosion due to entrenched consumer distress and escalating unemployment).

This model constructs the WACC utilizing the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) integrated with South African sovereign risk adjustments and private market illiquidity premiums.

*Methodological derivation:* The foundation of the Cost of Equity (Ke) rests upon the risk-free rate, anchored by the current South African 10-year government bond yield, which stabilized at 8.93% in March 2026\.46 The Equity Risk Premium (ERP) utilizes Professor Aswath Damodaran's authoritative methodology, which isolates a mature market premium (4.23%) and adds an adjusted South African country risk premium (3.90%) derived from sovereign CDS spreads and Moody's Ba2 ratings, yielding a robust 8.13%.48 While Famous Brands exhibits an artificially suppressed 5-year historical beta of 0.31, aligning with the broader South African Consumer Discretionary sector beta of 0.70 provides a more structurally sound, forward-looking proxy for systemic risk.49 Applying the CAPM framework (8.93% \+ 0.70 \* 8.13%) yields a baseline equity cost of 14.62%. The addition of a 2.50% size and illiquidity premium reflects the transition from a highly liquid public listing to absolute private control, resulting in a final Cost of Equity (Ke) of 17.12%.

The pre-tax Cost of Debt (Kd) is constructed by layering the Cbonds South Africa Corporate High Yield T-spread (currently 283 basis points) atop the 8.93% risk-free rate, culminating in a pre-tax debt cost of 11.76%.46 Adjusting this for the statutory 27.0% corporate tax shield yields an after-tax Cost of Debt of 8.58%. Blending these inputs across the target capital structure (derived from the current 86.6% Debt-to-Equity proxy, equating to a 46.4% Debt / 53.6% Equity weighting) produces the aggregate discount rate.

* **Low:** 12.00% (Assuming substantial sovereign risk upgrades, aggressive SARB interest rate cuts significantly reducing the 10-year yield, and highly successful post-acquisition deleveraging).

## **Q23: Exit EV/EBITDA multiple**

The determination of the terminal exit EV/EBITDA multiple requires a delicate balance between currently depressed JSE public equity valuations and the structural premiums historically commanded in private equity take-private transactions.

Over the trailing twelve months leading into March 2026, Famous Brands (JSE: FBR) has traded at a heavily discounted EV/EBITDA multiple of 6.8x to 6.9x.51 This valuation is not an isolated anomaly; it is tightly clustered with its closest domestic operational peer, Spur Corporation (JSE: SUR), which commands an identical 6.9x multiple.51 Broader hospitality and consumer discretionary stocks on the JSE, such as City Lodge Hotels (7.6x) and Tsogo Sun (8.4x), operate at a slight premium but ultimately confirm that the entire domestic consumer landscape is suffering from severe multiple compression relative to historical norms.49 For extreme contrast, global QSR behemoths such as Restaurant Brands International and Yum\! Brands operate at multiples frequently exceeding 12.0x, highlighting the severe emerging market discount currently applied to South African assets.

The South African private equity landscape is navigating a complex cycle often termed the "Great Reset." While funds under management contracted slightly to ZAR 233.3 billion by late 2024, transaction intent remains exceptionally high for 2026\.53 The structural shift toward JSE delistings is increasingly viewed not as a symptom of economic crisis, but as a healthy maturation of the capital markets, allowing private operators to restructure and deploy capital away from the intense short-termism of public quarterly reporting.55 Empirical evaluations of South African M\&A transactions historically demonstrate that acquiring entities pay an average control premium of 30% to 40% over undisturbed public trading prices.56

Applying a conservative 35% control premium to the 6.8x public trading baseline establishes a floor take-private entry valuation of approximately 9.2x EV/EBITDA. A robust LBO model must assume that the exit multiple in Year 5 will re-rate slightly as macroeconomic conditions improve and structural supply chain efficiencies are realized under private ownership, but it must remain tethered to the reality of emerging market liquidity constraints.
