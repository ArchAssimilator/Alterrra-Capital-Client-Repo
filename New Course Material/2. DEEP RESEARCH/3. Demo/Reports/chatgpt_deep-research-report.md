# Famous Brands take‑private model assumptions benchmark pack

## Macro and sector backdrop

South Africa’s medium‑term macro foundation in the latest fiscal framework is “low growth, low inflation, gradually improving confidence”, with household demand expected to remain the key swing factor for discretionary eat‑out and takeaway spend. entity["country","South Africa","southern africa country"]’s National Treasury expects real GDP growth of **1.4 per cent in 2025** and **1.6 per cent in 2026**, with growth projected to **average 1.8 per cent from 2026 to 2028** and reach **2.0 per cent by 2028**. citeturn35view0 The same forecast set implies a “soft landing” inflation path: **CPI inflation 3.2 per cent (2025), 3.4 per cent (2026), 3.3 per cent (2027), 3.2 per cent (2028)**, with upside risk explicitly linked to food prices (notably meat) and disease outbreaks. citeturn35view0

Household demand is expected to normalise after a stronger 2025: Treasury projects household consumption growth of **3.1 per cent in 2025**, slowing to **1.8 per cent in 2026**, and averaging **2.0 per cent over 2026–2028**, supported by (among other factors) “a stable power supply”, easing inflation and better sentiment. citeturn35view0 For a franchised QSR/casual dining ecosystem, this is consistent with “value‑seeking” consumer behaviour: frequency can be pressured, but well‑positioned brands often protect volumes via promotional architectures and menu engineering (especially where delivery and drive‑thru penetration is higher). The macro base case is therefore supportive of **low‑ to mid‑single‑digit nominal sales growth**, but does not underpin a high‑growth narrative without additional catalysts such as outlet expansion, market‑share gains or material recovery in real disposable income. citeturn35view0

On the operational side, the most material South Africa‑specific swing variable for foodservice over 2024–2026 has been the degree of load‑shedding and the knock‑on effects on operating hours, customer convenience and diesel/generator costs. Famous Brands’ own FY 2025 commentary links improved conditions to cost relief: it references “progress in halting load shedding”, moderating food inflation, and separately notes that the “cessation of load shedding” reduced franchisee financial relief and lowered diesel/generator maintenance costs, partially offset by higher electricity tariffs. citeturn17view0 Treasury’s macro narrative similarly embeds “improved electricity availability” and a “stable power supply” as supportive for confidence and consumption. citeturn35view0

Food inflation remains a pivotal sensitivity for restaurant affordability and for input costs (directly and via supplier pricing). Statistics South Africa reported **headline food inflation at 4.4 per cent in January 2026**, with notable moderation in some cereal products and oils & fats relative to late‑2025 levels. citeturn16search16 Treasury explicitly flags higher food prices (particularly meat) as a driver of the 2026 inflation uptick. citeturn35view0 For modelling, the implication is that **input cost pressure is not “gone”; it is simply less broad‑based than in peak‑inflation years**, and remains vulnerable to disease, exchange‑rate swings and electricity‑linked cost escalation. citeturn35view0turn16search16
