## 1. Introduction

In 2015, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommended that all people living with HIV (PLHIV) start antiretroviral therapy (ART) irrespective of clinical or immune status. However, despite this progress, **up to half the people living with HIV continue to present to care with Advanced HIV Disease (AHD).**

**WHO definition of AHD** (for adults and adolescents aged ≥5 years): CD4 cell count of less than 200 cells/mm³ or WHO clinical stage 3 or 4 disease.

AHD includes:
- People presenting to care for the first time following an HIV diagnosis
- People with treatment failure and consequent decline in CD4 count
- Individuals who had previously initiated ART and are re-engaging with care after a period of ART interruption (should be assessed for AHD)

People presenting with AHD are at high risk of death, even after starting ART, with risk increasing with decreasing CD4 count — especially with CD4 < 100 cells/mm³. AHD is also associated with increased healthcare costs, opportunistic infections, IRIS, incomplete immune reconstitution, higher viral reservoirs, and higher inflammation.

**Leading causes of mortality among adults with AHD globally** include tuberculosis (TB), severe bacterial infections, cryptococcal disease, histoplasmosis, toxoplasmosis, and Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP).

The aim of this document is to provide clinical guidance in the care of hospitalised patients with AHD, who often present with complex problems and multiple opportunistic infections.

## 2. Renal Impairment

*Primary Author: Jeremy Nel*

### Background

Patients with AHD commonly have abnormal creatinine levels. Serum creatinine **should not be used as the sole indicator of renal failure** as it varies independently of renal function with muscle bulk. Instead, estimate the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) using:

- **Cockroft-Gault formula:** Uses sex, age, weight, and creatinine. More accurate for wasted patients.
- **CKD-EPI:** Uses sex, race, age, and creatinine. Often calculated automatically on lab reports. More accurate than Cockroft-Gault except in extremely wasted patients.

> **Note:** Dolutegravir inhibits creatinine secretion directly, without affecting renal function. A patient started on dolutegravir can expect their serum creatinine to rise (usually <25%) within the first few weeks. A rise of >20–25% or a rise after the first month should prompt workup for alternative causes.

### Is the Renal Dysfunction Acute or Chronic?

**Clues to differentiate AKI from CKD:**

| Clue | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Small kidneys (<9 cm) on ultrasound/CT | CKD |
| A previous normal creatinine within the last 3 months | AKI |
| Normal haemoglobin | AKI (99% of CKD patients have anaemia) |
| High serum phosphate | CKD |
| Oliguria | AKI (though end-stage CKD patients can also be oliguric) |

### Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)

**Common causes:**
- **Pre-renal AKI** — hypoperfusion (dehydration, sepsis). Responds to fluids.
- **Acute tubular necrosis (ATN)** — from ischaemia or toxins (NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, amphotericin B, tenofovir rarely)
- **Acute interstitial nephritis** — from rifampicin, cotrimoxazole (Bactrim), NSAIDs, herbal remedies

**Management principles for AKI:**
1. Give a trial of fluids if there is a suspicion of a pre-renal component (1 litre isotonic crystalloid over 30 minutes, then 3 litres/day thereafter; assess for fluid overload before each litre).
2. Stop all commonly nephrotoxic medications (NSAIDs, tenofovir, cotrimoxazole).
3. Dose-adjust any medications requiring adjustment in renal compromise (pyrazinamide, ethambutol).
4. Attempt to correct any metabolic derangements — pay particular attention to hyperkalaemia.
5. Treat the underlying cause (e.g., diarrhoea, pneumonia).
6. If urinary obstruction is suspected, obtain an urgent ultrasound.
7. Assess for the need for acute dialysis. Indications include: acidosis, hyperkalaemia, refractory pulmonary oedema, uraemic gastritis, uraemic pericarditis, or uraemic encephalopathy.

> **Note on tenofovir (TDF) in AKI:** TDF should be stopped as it may be contributing to AKI, but don't stop all antiretrovirals — change TDF to another ARV (e.g., abacavir). TDF is a very rare cause of AKI in the absence of a second insult; always consider other causes.

### Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD)

**Two main aspects to consider:**

**1. HIV-Associated Nephropathy (HIVAN)** — direct HIV infection of renal cells.

Typical features suggestive of HIVAN:
- Advanced HIV (high viral load, low CD4)
- Black race (strong association with APOL1 gene variant)
- Large or high to normal sized kidneys (10.5–15.5 cm)
- Nephrotic or subnephrotic proteinuria
- Bland urinary sediment
- Absence of significant oedema or hypertriglyceridaemia

Management of HIVAN:
- Initiate antiretroviral therapy urgently (to prevent rapid decline in GFR)
- Give an ACE-inhibitor if the patient is significantly proteinuric (>1g/day); monitor potassium and GFR for 2 weeks; stop if GFR falls >20%

**2. General management of CKD:**
- Control blood pressure (<130/80), HbA1c (<7.0%), HIV viral load (undetectable)
- Counsel the patient about avoiding nephrotoxic drugs (e.g., NSAIDs)
- Dose-adjust medications as needed
- Refer to nephrologist if GFR < 30 ml/min

> **Recommended first-line ART regimen in CKD:** Lamivudine (or emtricitabine) + abacavir + dolutegravir (available as a fixed dose combination). It is no longer believed to be strictly necessary to dose adjust lamivudine in renal failure.

**ART dosage adjustments in renal failure:**

| Drug | CrCl 10–50 mL/min | CrCl <10 mL/min | Haemodialysis |
|---|---|---|---|
| TDF | 300 mg 48–72 hourly (depending on GFR) | 300 mg weekly | 300 mg 48–72 hourly |
| TAF | 25 mg daily | Unknown | Unchanged |
| ABC | Unchanged | Unchanged | Unchanged |
| 3TC | 150 mg daily | 50 mg daily | 50 mg first dose, then 25 mg daily |
| AZT | Unchanged | 300 mg daily | 300 mg daily |
| NNRTIs | Unchanged | Unchanged | Unchanged |
| PIs | Unchanged | Unchanged | Unchanged |
| InSTIs | Unchanged | Unchanged | Unchanged |

## 3. Diarrhoea

*Primary Author: David Stead*

### Background

Diarrhoea is common in AHD, affecting 40–80% of HIV-infected people not on ART. Chronic diarrhoea caused 10–25% of early mortality on ART in a systematic review of low- and middle-income countries. For this guideline: **acute diarrhoea = <2 weeks; chronic diarrhoea = ≥2 weeks.**

### Key Features on History and Examination

**History:**
- Duration, severity, and previous episodes
- Stool consistency; presence of mucous or blood
- Constitutional symptoms: fever, night sweats, weight loss
- Potential drug causes: protease inhibitors, recent antibiotic use
- Recent travel or possible contaminated water exposure
- HIV control: current ARVs, last viral load and CD4

**Opportunistic infections causing diarrhoea occur mostly with:**
- CD4 < 200: Cryptosporidium and Cystoisospora belli
- CD4 < 50: Cytomegalovirus (CMV) and Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC)

**Examination:** Hydration status, blood pressure, pulse, temperature, signs of TB (adenopathy, chest signs), abdominal examination, fundoscopy for features of CMV retinitis.

### Infectious Causes of Diarrhoea in AHD

| Pathogen | Bowel | Diagnosis | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cryptosporidium | Small | Stool modified auramine | Nil (initiate ART urgently) |
| Cystoisospora belli | Small | Stool modified auramine | Co-trimoxazole (80/400mg) 4 tablets orally 12 hourly for 10 days; ciprofloxacin 500mg 12 hourly if CTX-allergic |
| Microsporidia spp. | Small | Stool calcofluor stain | Albendazole 400mg orally 12 hourly for 4 weeks |
| Giardia | Small | Stool microscopy | Metronidazole 400mg orally 8 hourly for 5 days or 2g daily for 3 days |
| Amoebiasis | Large | Stool microscopy | Metronidazole 800mg orally 8 hourly for 10 days |
| Salmonella typhi | Large | Stool culture | Fluoroquinolone |
| Shigella | Large | Stool culture | Fluoroquinolone |
| Campylobacter | Large | Stool culture | Macrolide |
| C. difficile | Large | Stool toxin, GDH & C.diff PCR | Metronidazole 400mg orally 8 hourly for 10–14 days; second line: Vancomycin 125mg orally 6 hourly |
| M.TB | Small | Urine LAM, culture/biopsy/ultrasound | Anti-TB therapy |
| M. avium-complex | Small | Blood/bone marrow TB culture | Azithromycin + Ethambutol |
| CMV | Large | Biopsy | Ganciclovir |
| HIV | Small | Exclude other causes | Antiretrovirals |

**Non-infectious causes:** Protease inhibitors (lopinavir/ritonavir > darunavir/r > atazanavir/r), malabsorption syndromes, malignancies (Non-Hodgkin's Lymphoma, Kaposi sarcoma).

### Initial Investigations

- **Bloods:** Electrolytes, urea, creatinine, FBC, HIV viral load and CD4 (if not done in last 3 months)
- **Blood cultures:** Bacterial (if suspecting dysentery with pyrexia); mycobacterial (if disseminated TB or MAC suspected)
- **Stool analysis:** Acute — stool MC&S if mucous or blood, C.diff testing if risk factors; Chronic — stool MC&S plus modified auramine stain, up to 3 sequential stool samples
- **Urine TB-LAM test** if chronic diarrhoea with constitutional symptoms
- **Radiology:** CXR if pulmonary TB considered; abdominal ultrasound if disseminated TB suspected
- **Endoscopy:** Not an initial investigation but useful for patients with non-resolving symptoms after negative initial stool analyses. Yields an additional diagnosis in 30–70% of cases.

### Common Pitfalls

- Mis-diagnosing a chronic/intermittent diarrhoea as an acute episode
- Failing to send stool samples for appropriate stains
- Discharging a severe cryptosporidium case after ivi rehydration without initiating ART in-hospital
- Overuse of loperamide with a protease inhibitor (risk of severe ileus; maximum 1 tablet 12 hourly)

## 4. Cerebral Space Occupying Lesions

*Primary Author: Tom Boyles*

### Background

Space occupying lesions in the brain are a common problem in patients with AHD. Patients usually present with new onset or changing patterns of seizures, or with a gradually worsening focal neurological deficit such as hemiplegia.

### Differential Diagnosis

- TB (most common)
- Toxoplasmosis (second most common in the absence of pathognomonic CT signs)
- Pyogenic abscess
- Neurocysticercosis (NCC)
- Primary CNS lymphoma
- Secondary brain tumour
- Cryptococcoma
- Nocardia
- Syphilitic gumma

> **Note:** Neurocysticercosis has pathognomonic CT findings (multiple cysts with dot lesions). Beware of radiology CT reports that are confident about aetiologies other than NCC.

### If CT Scan Has Been Performed

If there are no pathognomonic signs on CT, the next 2 most common diagnoses are TB and toxoplasmosis. Suggested management:
- Take a full history and examination
- Investigations: CXR, HIV viral load and CD4, serum toxoplasmosis IgG, serum CrAg
- Consider serum syphilis serology

### If CT Scan Has Not Been Performed

If a CT scan cannot be done or is unavailable:
- A lumbar puncture may be performed if the patient does not have signs of raised intracranial pressure (no papilloedema, focal neurology, or altered consciousness)
- Consider empiric treatment for the most likely diagnoses based on clinical presentation and epidemiology

### Management

- **Empiric treatment for TB + toxoplasmosis** is the recommended first-line approach in Southern Africa when CT is non-diagnostic
- Anticonvulsants for seizure management
- Steroids may be indicated in specific circumstances (see Chapter 9)
- Monitor for IRIS

## 5. Acute and Chronic Meningitis

*Primary Author: Jeremy Nel*

### Background

Meningitis is a common and life-threatening presentation in patients with AHD. The most common causes in Southern Africa are:
- **Cryptococcal meningitis (CM)** — most common
- **Tuberculosis meningitis (TBM)**
- **Bacterial meningitis** (less common in this context)
- **Viral meningitis** (including HIV itself)

### Approach to Meningitis

All patients with suspected meningitis should have a lumbar puncture (LP) unless contraindicated.

**CSF opening pressure** must be measured at LP — this is critical in cryptococcal meningitis management.

**CSF analysis:**
- Appearance, cell count, differential, protein, glucose (with simultaneous blood glucose)
- Gram stain and culture
- India ink stain
- CSF CrAg (cryptococcal antigen)
- TB tests: PCR, culture, ZN stain
- Syphilis serology if indicated

### Cryptococcal Meningitis (CM)

**Diagnosis:** CSF CrAg and/or India ink positive. Serum CrAg is highly sensitive (~99%) as a screening test.

**Management:**
- **Induction phase (2 weeks):** Amphotericin B (liposomal preferred where available) + flucytosine. If flucytosine unavailable, amphotericin B + fluconazole.
- **Consolidation phase (8 weeks):** Fluconazole 400 mg daily
- **Maintenance phase (at least 1 year):** Fluconazole 200 mg daily (continue until CD4 >200 and VL undetectable for >3 months)

**Management of raised intracranial pressure (ICP):** This is a major cause of early mortality.
- If opening pressure > 25 cm H₂O: perform therapeutic LP and remove enough CSF to bring pressure to ≤20 cm H₂O or reduce by 50%
- Serial therapeutic LPs may be needed daily until pressure normalises
- Do NOT use acetazolamide or mannitol (not effective and potentially harmful)

**ART initiation in CM:** Defer ART for 4–6 weeks after starting antifungal therapy (early ART is associated with increased mortality due to CM-IRIS).

### Tuberculosis Meningitis (TBM)

**Diagnosis:** Often difficult — CSF may show lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, low glucose. CSF TB PCR (Xpert MTB/RIF) is helpful.

**Management:**
- Standard anti-TB therapy for 9–12 months (not the standard 6-month regimen)
- Dexamethasone is indicated regardless of HIV status (reduces mortality)
- ART initiation: start after 4–8 weeks (early ART increases risk of paradoxical IRIS in TBM)

## 6. Respiratory Presentation

*Primary Author: Gary Maartens*

### Background

Respiratory presentations are extremely common in patients with AHD. The differential diagnosis is broad and presentations can overlap significantly.

### Key Respiratory Diagnoses in AHD

**Tuberculosis (TB):**
- Most common cause of respiratory presentation in AHD in Southern Africa
- Investigations: sputum Xpert MTB/RIF, sputum microscopy and culture, CXR, urine TB-LAM (particularly useful at low CD4 counts)
- Treatment: Standard RHEZ regimen (6 months)

**Pneumocystis jirovecii Pneumonia (PJP):**
- Common when CD4 < 200 cells/mm³
- Presents with progressive dyspnoea, non-productive cough, fever; hypoxia often more severe than clinical signs suggest
- Investigations: CXR (bilateral interstitial infiltrates), LDH (elevated), serum β-D-glucan, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) with PCR
- Treatment: High-dose cotrimoxazole (trimethoprim 15–20 mg/kg/day + sulfamethoxazole 75–100 mg/kg/day) for 21 days; add corticosteroids if pO₂ < 70 mmHg or A-a gradient > 35 mmHg
- Prophylaxis: Continue cotrimoxazole prophylaxis (CTX) until CD4 > 200 on ART

**Bacterial Pneumonia:**
- Common at all CD4 counts; recurrent pneumonia is an AIDS-defining illness
- Presentation: acute fever, productive cough, lobar consolidation on CXR
- Treatment: Standard empiric antibiotics per local guidelines

**Other:**
- Kaposi sarcoma (pulmonary)
- Lymphoma
- Pulmonary hypertension

### Approach to Hypoxic Patient with AHD

1. Assess severity of hypoxia and need for supplemental oxygen
2. Take a thorough history (duration of symptoms, character of cough, fever pattern, prior TB treatment, current ART, CD4 and VL)
3. CXR, sputum Xpert MTB/RIF, urine TB-LAM
4. Blood cultures if febrile
5. Serum LDH if PJP suspected
6. Consider bronchoscopy and BAL if initial investigations non-diagnostic

## 7. Altered Mental State

*Primary Author: Tom Boyles*

### Background

Altered mental state in a patient with AHD may be due to a wide variety of causes. Causes can be divided into:
- **Intracranial causes:** Meningitis (cryptococcal, TB, bacterial, viral), cerebral SOL (see Chapter 4), HIV encephalopathy/dementia, progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML)
- **Systemic/metabolic causes:** Septic encephalopathy, hepatic encephalopathy, renal failure, hyponatraemia, hypoglycaemia, drug toxicity (especially antiretrovirals and anti-TB drugs)

### Approach

All patients with altered mental state and AHD require:
- Thorough history from a collateral source if possible
- Vital signs including blood glucose
- Blood tests: FBC, electrolytes, urea, creatinine, ALT, LFTs, blood cultures, HIV VL and CD4
- Lumbar puncture — unless contraindicated by signs of raised ICP. If LP is delayed, empiric treatment for the most common causes should be started.
- CT brain if focal neurology, signs of raised ICP, or if LP results do not explain the presentation

**Serum CrAg** should be checked in all patients with altered mental state and AHD — cryptococcal meningitis commonly presents with minimal neck stiffness.

### HIV-Associated Neurocognitive Disorders (HAND) and HIV Encephalopathy

- Often a diagnosis of exclusion
- Mild neurocognitive impairment to HIV-associated dementia
- Management: initiation or optimisation of ART is the primary intervention

## 8. Patient Deteriorating Despite Treatment for an Opportunistic Infection

*Primary Author: Gary Maartens*

### Background

A patient with AHD who is deteriorating despite apparently adequate treatment for an OI is a challenging and common clinical scenario. The key differential diagnoses are:

1. **Incorrect diagnosis** — the wrong OI (or additional OI) has been identified and the patient is not being treated for the actual cause
2. **Inadequate treatment** — correct diagnosis but incorrect drug, dose, or duration; adherence issues; drug malabsorption
3. **Drug resistance** — TB drug resistance is most clinically significant
4. **Treatment-limiting adverse drug reaction** — drug-induced liver injury (DILI), drug rash, etc.
5. **Immune Reconstitution Inflammatory Syndrome (IRIS)** — particularly if ART has been recently started or is being started

### Approach

- Revisit the original diagnosis — are you treating the right OI?
- Consider additional OIs — patients with AHD commonly have more than one OI simultaneously
- Review adherence and drug absorption
- Consider drug resistance — TB drug sensitivity testing is essential
- Assess for drug toxicity — LFTs, FBC, electrolytes
- Consider IRIS if ART was recently initiated

### IRIS

IRIS is an inflammatory reaction to an OI following ART initiation. It can mimic OI treatment failure. Types:
- **Paradoxical IRIS:** Patient was being treated for a known OI and deteriorates after ART initiation
- **Unmasking IRIS:** A subclinical OI becomes apparent after ART initiation

Management of IRIS: Treat the underlying OI, continue ART (except in life-threatening IRIS), consider NSAIDs or corticosteroids for severe inflammatory symptoms.

## 9. Use of Steroids in Advanced HIV Disease

*Primary Author: Gary Maartens*

### Key Principles

Steroids play an important but specific role in AHD management. Inappropriate use of steroids can be fatal (e.g., in undiagnosed cryptococcal meningitis).

### Indications for Steroids in AHD

| Condition | Steroid | Dose and Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Tuberculosis meningitis | Dexamethasone | 0.4 mg/kg/day for 4 weeks then taper over 4 weeks |
| PJP with hypoxia (pO₂ <70 mmHg) | Prednisone | 40 mg twice daily days 1–5, then 40 mg daily days 6–10, then 20 mg daily days 11–21 |
| TB-IRIS (severe) | Prednisone | 1.5 mg/kg/day for 2 weeks, then 0.75 mg/kg/day for 2 weeks |
| CM-IRIS (severe) | Prednisone | Use with caution; evidence is limited |

### Contraindications / Cautions

- Steroids are **not recommended** in cryptococcal meningitis (except for CM-IRIS under specialist supervision)
- Steroids are **not recommended** for paradoxical TB-IRIS in the CNS (TBM-IRIS) without specialist guidance
- Always rule out undiagnosed OIs (particularly fungal) before starting steroids
- Kaposi sarcoma can be dramatically worsened by steroids
