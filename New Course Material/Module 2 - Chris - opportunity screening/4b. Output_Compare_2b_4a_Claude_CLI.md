## Overall Verdict

**Version 1 is the better screening document for a PE investment team at first-pass stage.** It is more disciplined in its epistemic posture, consistently distinguishing between what is known, what is missing, and what is inferred. It reaches a more credible and appropriately hedged recommendation — "potential candidate, needs clarification" — which is exactly the right call given the evidence available from a public annual report alone. Its structure maps directly onto the staged PE screening framework requested, and every section closes with a "provisional assessment" that is honest about evidential limits.

Version 2 is a more complete and impressive-looking document, but it overshoots its evidential base at almost every turn. It presents illustrative deal structures, IRR tables, a 100-day value creation plan, and a DD workplan as if a transaction is already in motion — when there is no disclosed sale process, no agreed terms, no access to management, and no quality-of-earnings work done. The gap between the two versions is meaningful: Version 1 is an honest first-pass screen; Version 2 is a dressed-up IC memo that borrows the authority of a later-stage document without the underlying evidence to support it.

## Key Contradictions or Accuracy Concerns

**Internal contradiction in Version 2 (material):**
The executive summary states headline returns of 18–22% IRR in the base case, but the base case model in Section VII produces a MOIC of ~1.9x and IRR of ~13% at 10x entry / 10x exit. These figures cannot both be right. This is a fundamental modelling credibility issue.

**Internal contradiction in Version 2 (material):**
Version 2 repeatedly characterises Famous Brands as an "asset-light" franchisor with royalty-driven earnings, but its own revenue segmentation table shows logistics at 65% of revenue and franchise/royalty fees at only 12%. The two claims are in direct tension and Version 2 does not adequately reconcile them — it notes the issue in a footnote but then continues framing the thesis around asset-light economics.

**Cross-version factual discrepancy (minor):**
Version 1 describes the UK margin as having "fallen to 5.4%" and revenue "down 18%." Version 2 reports UK revenue of R132m vs R161m prior year, consistent with an 18% decline, but does not explicitly state the UK operating margin, making cross-check incomplete.

**Potentially unsupported claim in Version 2:**
The statement that the dividend has never been interrupted over 30 years of listing is not validated by the annual report and is potentially inaccurate given the GBK impairment period. Version 1 makes no such claim.

**Overreach in Version 2:**
The royalty rate range of "5–8% of restaurant turnover" is specific and material to the investment thesis but is not cited to any source in the public annual report. If this is an inference from industry norms rather than a disclosed fact, it should be labelled as such.

# **Version 2 still fails, even judged as an IC memo. And the failure is more damaging in that context, not less.**


---

### The core problem is the IRR contradiction

An IC memo lives or dies on the returns model. Version 2 presents 18–22% IRR in the executive summary and then produces 13% IRR in the body of the same document. That is not a rounding difference or a scenario labelling issue — it is a ~500–900 basis point gap in the headline return figure, in the same document, with no reconciliation.

Any partner or IC member who reads past the executive summary catches this immediately. At that point the document loses all credibility, regardless of how well the rest is written. In a real IC process, this would end the conversation before the investment merits are even debated.

---

### The "asset-light" framing collapses under its own evidence

Version 2 builds its entire investment thesis around asset-light franchisor economics and royalty-driven cash flow quality. It then provides a revenue table showing logistics at 65% of revenue and franchise royalties at 12%.

A genuine IC memo would resolve that tension — it would either argue that the logistics business is structurally complementary and should be valued on a different basis, or it would strip out the pass-through and present a clean royalty EBITDA number. Version 2 does neither. It notes the issue in a footnote and then continues arguing the asset-light thesis as if the footnote didn't exist. That is not analytical rigour — it is a presentation that hopes the reader won't notice.

---

### The valuation work is not IC-ready

For an IC memo to be credible, the valuation needs to be grounded in something. Version 2 uses an EBITDA figure derived backwards from the net debt / EBITDA ratio, explicitly labelled as implied rather than disclosed. It then builds a full LBO model, sources and uses table, and sensitivity grid on that derived number — without a quality-of-earnings adjustment, without a segment-level EBITDA split, and without any normalisation for the AME losses, UK drag, or Signature Brands losses.

The R1.121bn EBITDA used as the entry point almost certainly overstates what a buyer would underwrite, because it includes loss-making segments that a sponsor would either carve out or model separately. An IC memo that doesn't address this is not a returns analysis — it is arithmetic performed on an uncleaned number.

---

### The deal structure section creates legal risk exposure

Version 2 describes a Scheme of Arrangement structure, Competition Commission process, B-BBEE ownership restructuring, and franchise change-of-control notifications — all in the IC memo — without any legal or regulatory input. For a public-company transaction in South Africa, these are not background colour. They are execution conditions that could each independently kill the deal. Presenting them as a checklist rather than as assessed risks with probability and timeline estimates is not IC-standard.

---

### Where Version 2 would genuinely win in an IC context

To be fair: if this were a real IC process — meaning there was an NDA signed, management access obtained, a QoE completed, and legal counsel engaged — Version 2's *structure* is the right template. The sections are in the right order. The competitive landscape, market dynamics, and brand portfolio sections are well-organised. The DD workplan and legal considerations checklist are exactly what an IC memo should include. The B-BBEE section is genuinely strong and would be IC-ready with a proper transformation adviser's input.

So Version 2 would win on **structure and completeness** if the underlying evidence existed to support it.

---

### The verdict

Version 2 fails as an IC memo for the same reason it fails as a screen, just more visibly: it presents the format of a later-stage document without the evidentiary foundation. At screen stage, that is a discipline problem. At IC stage, it is a credibility problem. The IRR contradiction alone would not survive a competent IC process, and the asset-light / logistics revenue contradiction would not survive a first read from a partner who knows the business.

**Version 1 remains the more honest document. Version 2 is a better-looking document that a capable IC would pick apart in under ten minutes.**
